# MAANG-Style Interview Prep

Turn a resume + job description + target company into a rehearsal-ready prep package. The candidate should walk into the interview having *practiced out loud* from concrete, personalized material — not generic advice they have to adapt on the fly.

## Why this exists

Most interview prep fails for one of two reasons: the candidate has good stories but hasn't mapped them to what the interviewer actually cares about (the JD), or they've mapped it correctly but the answers are still mentally-drafted rather than rehearsed, so they come out clunky live. This skill forces both: every step below ends with a **written artifact** the candidate can read aloud and time, not just an explanation.

## Required inputs

Before starting, collect:
1. **Job description** (full text)
2. **Resume** (full text or pasted content)
3. **Target company** and, if known, the specific product/team
4. **Optional**: interviewer name(s) or LinkedIn URLs, target role level, language/tone/length constraints, relocation or comp sensitivities

If any of #1–#3 is missing, ask for it before starting — don't guess at a JD or fabricate resume content. If the resume is thin on a section, ask the candidate for 1–2 more examples rather than padding with generic filler.

**Golden rule to enforce throughout:** never invent experience that isn't in the candidate's resume or their own words. Where evidence or a metric is missing, insert a clearly-labeled placeholder like `[metric needed — ask candidate]` rather than fabricating a number. A confident-sounding invented statistic is worse than an honest gap, because it can fall apart under a follow-up question.

## Workflow

Work through these steps in order. Each one produces a labeled section of the final prep document — write the artifact as you go rather than only describing what it would contain.

### 1. Analyze the job description

Sort every concrete phrase in the JD into exactly three buckets:
- **What you'll do** — daily responsibilities, projects, stakeholders, ownership scope
- **Skills needed** — hard skills, tools, techniques (SQL, experiment design, executive presentation, etc.)
- **Domain knowledge** — industry/product/customer-segment familiarity (e-commerce, healthcare compliance, mobile ecosystems, etc.)

Discard vague corporate filler that isn't demonstrable ("fast learner," "passion for excellence") — keep only language the candidate could point to a real example for. From this table, pull the **top 5–8 must-prove requirements**: the things an interviewer is most likely to probe on. Note any requirement where the resume shows no clear evidence — flag it now so Step 2 can look for a fix.

Output: a three-column requirement table + the top 5–8 must-prove list.

### 2. Build the signature-project bank (STAR/CARL source material)

Surface 5–6 candidate projects or achievements from the resume (mostly work projects; 1–2 strong personal/volunteer examples are fine if they show real leadership or impact). Build a mapping matrix — JD requirement (rows) × project (columns) — marking where each project convincingly demonstrates that requirement.

A strong signature project usually has: relevance to multiple JD requirements, real scope or ambiguity, cross-functional involvement, a measurable result, and a genuine lesson learned. Recommend **3–4 primary projects** (broadest JD coverage) and 1–2 backups for variety across a multi-interview loop, so the candidate isn't reusing the exact same story twice.

Also map these same projects against seven common behavioral sub-themes so every theme has at least one owner: **problem solving, leadership, teamwork, resilience, integrity, decision-making, communication/interpersonal**. One strong project can usually answer 2–3 of these themes if reframed — same underlying facts, different opening context, emphasized action, and highlighted lesson (see Step 5).

Output: JD × project matrix, a primary/backup recommendation, and a project × behavioral-theme map.

### 3. Master the resume (resume-based questions)

Draft three scripts the candidate can read aloud and time:

1. **"Walk me through your resume" / "Tell me about yourself"** — a ~90-second **Present → Past → Future** answer: Present = current role + one recent headline achievement with a number; Past = how prior roles built the relevant skills, kept selective rather than a bullet re-read; Future = why this specific next role is the logical continuation. Flag it explicitly if it runs long when read aloud.
2. **"Tell me about your experience at Company X"** — one-sentence role summary → rough time-split across responsibilities ("~40% on A, ~40% on B, ~20% on C") → 1–2 headline accomplishments.
3. **"Tell me something not on your resume"** — one genuine personal interest or activity with a real, specific story (not a generic hobby line) that reveals a trait relevant to the role.

Also produce a **resume interrogation list**: for every bullet point, a ready answer to *why did you take this on*, *what were you personally accountable for*, *what impact did you drive and how*, *what would you do differently in hindsight*. This is what survives a sharp interviewer's follow-up probes.

### 4. Research the company / product area

Build a brief covering: how the company/product makes money and its rough revenue mix, strategic moves from the last 3–6 months, main competitors and how this company differentiates, and 2–3 open challenges or growth bets the team is likely focused on. For product/business roles, go deeper on user journey, growth drivers, monetization, partnerships, and likely metrics the team is measured on.

Pull from the company's own site/blog, recent reputable news coverage, public filings if applicable, and (for private companies) funding/news databases. If web research tools are available, use them — don't rely on stale memory for "recent" company news. Time-box each spoken answer to roughly 30–90 seconds; rewrite for concision if it runs long. Close with 2–3 candidate points of view (not just facts) and 2–3 interview-ready questions this research suggests.

For company/product-knowledge questions specifically, structure answers as: direct point of view → evidence from the research → business/product reasoning → connection to the target role → a practical suggestion or question. Avoid generic praise — a strong answer shows the candidate understands tradeoffs and how the role fits into the bigger picture.

### 5. Behavioral answers with CARL

Use **CARL**, not STAR: **C**ontext → **A**ction → **R**esult → **L**earning. Context collapses STAR's Situation+Task into 1–2 efficient sentences, freeing more airtime for what the candidate actually did, achieved, and learned — Learning is the step most candidates skip entirely, which makes it the easiest differentiator to add.

For each of the 3–4 primary projects from Step 2, against each behavioral theme it's mapped to, draft a CARL script:
- **Context** — 1–2 sentences of setup only (who/what/why it mattered). Don't over-invest time here.
- **Action** — first-person and specific ("I," not "we") — makes individual contribution unambiguous.
- **Result** — quantified where possible; if no hard number exists, use a clearly-labeled placeholder or honest qualitative outcome rather than inventing a figure.
- **Learning** — a genuine reflection: what worked, what you'd change, how it shaped later behavior. This should sound like real judgment, not a template filled in mechanically.

Where one project answers multiple themes, vary the opening context, the emphasized action, and the highlighted lesson each time — keep the underlying facts consistent but don't reuse identical wording. Target each spoken answer at under ~3 minutes.

### 6. Motivation answers ("Why this company/role?")

Pick exactly three reasons, one from each category: (1) the company/role's mission or impact, (2) a specific day-to-day responsibility that genuinely excites the candidate, (3) a cultural trait (mentorship, autonomy, craftsmanship). For each, attach one personal, specific detail from the candidate's own history — rewrite anything generic enough that another candidate could say it verbatim.

Explicitly avoid compensation, prestige, or "it's a big name" as a stated reason — these read as superficial. Target under ~2 minutes spoken.

### 7. Strengths, weaknesses, and questions for the interviewer

- **Greatest strength** — pick one that maps directly to a Step 1 must-prove requirement; prepare a one-line version and a longer version with one concrete example.
- **Greatest weakness** — pick something real but non-disqualifying (difficulty delegating, impatience with delays, over-investing time in favorite work) — never a disguised humble-brag ("I work too hard"). Structure as: name it as an area of improvement → one concrete example → a specific, ongoing action taken to address it, ideally with early evidence it's working.
- **"Any questions for me?"** — prepare 2–3 questions mixing: (a) company/role/team-strategy questions grounded in the Step 4 research (not something a website could answer), and (b) a question about the interviewer's own background or experience. Never propose a question that's trivially Google-able.

### 8. Research the interviewing team (if names are known)

For each named interviewer, review their public LinkedIn profile only (never suggest sending a connection request beforehand) and note genuine commonalities (schools, past companies, shared interests) plus what's likely top-of-mind for their role, inferred from Step 4. Tailor the Step 7 interviewer-questions list to seniority — more strategic for senior/VP interviewers, more execution-focused for individual contributors.

Separately, suggest the candidate reach out to 2–3 people on the broader hiring team (not the actual interviewers) for informal 20–30 minute chats before the interview — one of the highest-leverage, most commonly skipped moves, since it surfaces real team culture/priorities that public research can't.

### 9. Assemble the final package and rehearsal checklist

Compile everything into the output format below, then confirm each item:

- [ ] JD requirement table + top 5–8 must-prove list (Step 1)
- [ ] Signature-project matrix covering all 7 behavioral themes (Step 2)
- [ ] Resume scripts drafted, "walk me through your resume" timed under ~90 seconds (Step 3)
- [ ] Company/product research brief complete, with a same-week news check flagged (Step 4)
- [ ] CARL answer bank drafted, each script under ~3 minutes (Step 5)
- [ ] Motivation answer with 3 personalized reasons (Step 6)
- [ ] Strength + weakness answers, 2–3 interviewer questions (Step 7)
- [ ] Interviewer/team research, if names were provided (Step 8)
- [ ] Any `[metric needed]` or `[example needed]` placeholders listed as an explicit to-do for the candidate

**Enforce this throughout, not just at the end:** every answer should be rehearsed *out loud*, not just read silently — polished-sounding drafts often come out awkward the first time spoken, so tell the candidate to budget rehearsal time as heavily as prep time.

Save this as `10-Action/Internship/Interview-Prep/<Company>-<Role-slug>-YYYY-MM-DD.md` inside the vault (create the `Interview-Prep/` folder if it doesn't exist yet), with the frontmatter above filled in — the `company`/`role`/`created` fields feed the Career Toolkit hub's auto-listing, so don't skip them. This gives the candidate a persistent file to keep updating and rehearsing from, rather than only presenting it in chat.
